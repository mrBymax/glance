# Zero-context reviews of the design document

Date: 2026-11-13. Each reviewer was a fresh agent with no knowledge of the team's conversation. Each one read `CHALLENGE.md` and `docs/superpowers/specs/2026-11-13-meta-glance-design.md`. The reports below are the reviewers' words, lightly formatted. They are input for the team, not decisions.

## Rival team

### Part 1: as the rival

Weaknesses:

- Identity is a guess. The stream cannot read a barcode or small print, so each price and allergen warning depends on the look of the package. Twelve products hide the variants.
- A sighted shopper holds, waits about 2 seconds (not measured), and nods. A scanner is faster. Meta already announced product and price recognition.
- Frames with bystanders go to Gemini for the whole shop. Face blur is P1 item 4, and a late M1 cuts all of P1.
- No blind person has tried it. Nothing guides one to the product on the shelf or to the kiosk code.
- "The store installs nothing" is false: the kiosk must show the code and accept the cart. A shopper with prescription lenses cannot wear lent glasses. The EU Accessibility Act lets current terminals stay up to 20 years, so no law forces the change.

The rival's build: glasses that guide the finger of a blind person on touchscreens that exist today, such as self-checkouts. One first-person view holds the screen and the finger. The camera runs only after a tap. A blind tester uses it on Saturday.

- Demo moment: a Meta judge wears the glasses, closes their eyes, and buys a real snack from a real machine.
- Line: "No store has to change its checkout. The camera runs for ten seconds, not for your whole shop."

Planted questions:

1. "Your stream cannot read small print. How do you tell the gluten-free oat drink from the regular one? Who is liable when the voice misses the allergen?"
2. "Your camera sends other shoppers, children too, to Google for the whole shop. Is face blur in the build on this table?"
3. "I wear prescription lenses, and the battery streams for 30 minutes. Who gets the lent glasses, and who rebuilds the kiosk?"

Strengths that worry the rival:

- "Glance tells, nod buys" is a line that judges repeat. It quotes Meta's "Deliberate, not ambient".
- The state table, demo step 6, and a fallback for each beta capability answer the challenge text on errors and device state.
- The team discloses first: anti-goals, a labeled mock, measured numbers. That leaves little to expose.

### Part 2: as an adviser to the team

Actions, ranked, with the rival's estimate in person-hours:

1. Get a blind or low-vision tester for Saturday. Ask tonight: the organizers, the Meta mentors, the Finnish Federation of the Visually Impaired. Run the loop with eyes shut to find bugs, not as user evidence. Add spoken guidance to the kiosk code. 5 hours.
2. Lead with blind and low-vision shoppers. Change the pilot to owners of the glasses, invited to the DAT beta channel. Delete "lent at the entrance" and "installs nothing". 1 hour.
3. Move face blur to P0. Cut the HUD to one simulator image. 3 hours.
4. Put two look-alike variants in the catalog. The voice says "I cannot tell which one. Turn it." 2 hours.
5. Protect the measurements: accuracy, time from glance to voice, false yes (walk, and talk at the kiosk), battery. 2 hours.
6. Judges cannot hear the glasses, so show captions on the kiosk screen. Test the nod on five heads. 2 hours.

To pay for these 15 hours, the rival would cut Italian, the spot check, the fingerprint step, and "Ask about a product".

Opening sentence for the pitch: "A blind shopper cannot read this label or use that touchscreen checkout, so we made the glasses the scanner: a glance tells, a nod buys."

## Meta judge

Basis: P0 and P1 as built, with a working nod. The tap fallback shares a Developer Center gate with the nod, so both can fail together.

Scores:

| Criterion | Score | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Creativity and use case | 6 | Grocery scanning is a common glasses hack, and Meta already announced price recognition and tests QR payment. The consent rule is original, and the rest is integration. |
| Technical execution | 7 | A working nod and tested device states beat the field. The hard problem stays open: 12 products in a prompt, one cloud call for each glance. |
| Real-world value and feasibility | 5 | The loop stops at the demo table and at about 30 minutes of battery. The pilot needs lent glasses that pair with a personal phone, and catalog, kiosk, and loss-check integration. |
| User experience | 8 | Redundant inputs, captions, haptics, and no countdown are the best the judge expects to see. No disabled shopper has used it. |

Average: 6.5.

Verdict: the build and the consent rule earn a top-ten place, not top three. The idea joins two features that Meta already announced, and the demo proves 12 products on a table, not a store. Most shoppers get a slower scanner, and the shoppers who gain most have not tried it.

Strongest points:

1. The consent rule: glance tells, nod buys, and a yes counts only for the thing in view. It matches "deliberate, not ambient".
2. The build handles glasses off or folded, a lost server, and a failed payment.
3. The pitch is honest: measured accuracy, latency, and false-yes numbers, a labeled demo payment, and clear platform asks.

Weakest points:

1. Recognition does not scale. The stream cannot read a barcode, so look-alike variants with different prices and allergens have no answer.
2. The pitch serves three audiences. The blind shopper gains most and has not tested it.
3. The purchase is deliberate, but the capture is ambient: the camera uploads up to two frames a second for the whole shop. Face blur ranks below the Display HUD.

Questions, hardest first:

1. Your stream cannot read a barcode. Among 30,000 products, how do you tell the oat drink from its barista edition, and who pays for a wrong match?
2. A handheld scanner reads the barcode in a second. Your loop needs about five. Which shopper is better off, and has that shopper tried it?
3. How is a camera that runs for the whole shop "deliberate, not ambient"? Why is face blur an extra?
4. I hear the price and bend to return the product to the bottom shelf. Is that a nod? I swap products and nod before the voice names the new one. The voice says only "Added". What did I buy?
5. We announced product and price recognition, and we test QR payment. What did you learn this weekend that we do not know?

The one change: build for one shopper, the blind or low-vision shopper who already owns the glasses. Cut the Display HUD and the Italian voice, and film one such shopper in the full loop. The baseline becomes "ask staff for help", not "a faster scanner", and the pilot needs no lent glasses. Use case, value, and experience each gain one or two points, which is top-three range.

## Sceptic

Summary: the full plan does not fit. A cut plan can. The sceptic tested nothing on hardware.

Holes, ranked:

1. P0 does not fit. Likelihood: high. It has 9 phone screens, 14 device states, and 3 web front ends, and `apps/` is empty. M2 is at most 18 hours away. Cut P0 to: glance speaks, yes adds, QR pays.
2. One gate controls nod, double tap, and voice. Likelihood: medium, but it removes "nod buys". The plan uses app ID `0`, and the SDK references require Developer Center enablement for Motion, Inputs, and Speech. If Developer Mode does not bypass that, the fallbacks in section 16 fail together. Test `addMotion` on the real glasses first.
3. Time on the glasses. Likelihood: high. One pair, and at most 30 minutes of stream for each charge. Checks, gesture tuning, and the 3-hour shoot all need it. Test 10 minutes with stream, Motion, and audio together. Use the Display as a second stream device.
4. "In view" comes from late, noisy recognitions. Likelihood: high. A late change of the pending product adds the wrong item, and "Added. Total..." does not name it. Fix: the intent carries the spoken product ID, and the server rejects a mismatch.
5. Demo step 6. Likelihood: high. A fold ends the session permanently. A stream that starts off the face pauses when worn, until a tap. Cut it from the take.
6. Pitch. Likelihood: high. The camera uploads 2 frames each second, and face blur is P1, while Meta asks for "Deliberate, not ambient". "Glasses lent at the entrance" ignores pairing through Meta AI. A blind shopper gets no aim hint for the kiosk code. Judges cannot hear open-ear audio. Fix: drop frames with faces, pitch a store-owned kit, play each clip on the kiosk laptop.
7. False yes. Likelihood: medium. Speech may hear "Nod to add" and "Nod to pay" from the speakers. A put-back on a low shelf looks like a nod. Cut voice commands. Record put-backs.
8. The kiosk code does not decode at 1 m. Likelihood: medium. Fix: a payload of 8 characters and a code of 25 cm.

Unsupported or wrong:

- "About 2 seconds" from glance to voice has no source. The sceptic estimates 2 to 4.
- The QR check: a URL with a database ID needs 33 to 37 modules, which gives about 3 pixels for each module at 1 m, and 2 after one resolution step down.
- The Inputs docs warn against a feature "that depends on any particular touchpad interaction reaching your app".
- Rule R2 keeps the nod window open while the product is in view. The research asks for a short window after a prompt.
- "Check 7" and "check 2" in the design follow the numbering of the research file, not the rows of section 16.
- The timeline omits the preliminary submission on Friday at 23:55.

Slower than planned:

- The Friday checks. The sample app covers stream and photo only. Expect 3 to 5 hours.
- The WebView bridge, the gesture thresholds, and rules R1 to R15 against live frames. Unit tests do not show flicker.
- Seat 4 owns video, deck, website, HUD, store, and glasses tests.

What the sceptic would cut now:

1. The WebView and Astryx phone app. Use one native screen.
2. All of P1 except the allergen guard, and also voice commands and the HUD.
3. Demo step 6, the shoot in a real store, and nonce rotation.

Evidence that would change the sceptic's mind:

- Within 2 hours: Motion reaches STARTED with app ID `0`, and the stream stays at 720 x 1280 during a clip.
- 100 frames over all 12 products: 95 percent correct, no wrong "identified", and p95 under 2.5 seconds at the venue.
- Two people, 15 minutes of walks and put-backs: zero false yes.
- A glance speaks on the real glasses by Saturday 13:00.

## Critic (interaction design and accessibility)

Issues, most severe first:

1. Pay step, worst for a blind shopper. The Connected screen of the kiosk lists no QR code, and the pay prompt can outlast the grace time of 4 seconds. A nod after the prompt then fails (R11), while the kiosk shows "Waiting for your nod" for 20 seconds (R12). A blind shopper also gets no cue to find the code. Fix: keep the code full-size on the Connected screen, start the grace time when the prompt ends, and add a spoken kiosk beacon.
2. Change of mind at the kiosk. To remove the crisps, the shopper holds them up. The product prompt (R6) and the checkout (R10) are both live. A nod adds or pays. A shake removes or cancels. Fix: a held product suspends checkout. Then the voice repeats the pay prompt with the new total.
3. Recognition error, blind shopper by touch. The glance line gives no brand or size, and "Added. Total four forty-nine." names no product, so a wrong match stays hidden. After a shake, R4 mutes that product, and no manual add exists. Fix: say "Added oat drink" with one attribute that a hand can check ("one-litre carton"). After a no, ask Gemini again without the rejected ID.
4. Two of one product, parent with a child. The cart drifts from the basket. R5 ignores the nod for the second carton in silence. A head shake at the child removes the product in the hand. Fix: on an ignored nod, say "Hold it up again to add another." A shake removes only within 4 seconds of "Added". Count false no in the walk test.
5. Deaf, no nod, or no speech in noise. The phone is the fallback, but a look at the phone moves the target out of view, and the grace time ends. A missed line needs a spoken "repeat" or the phone. Prompts still say "nod". Fix: a phone button acts on the card it sits on, with no view test. Add a touchpad swipe for repeat.
6. Deaf shopper, empty battery. No haptic exists for a pause or a stop, so in pocket mode the shopper nods at a stopped camera. A shop of 25 items can outlast the 30-minute stream, and R11 needs the glasses to pay. Fix: a repeated "camera off" haptic, and a kiosk scan with the phone camera.
7. Wheelchair user at the kiosk. A chair can stop beyond the 1 m that the QR check tests. R10 binds the first code that decodes, perhaps at the next kiosk. Fix: test from a chair at 1.5 m, and bind the largest code after 1.5 seconds in view.
8. First three minutes, tremor. The app asks for the head gesture setting before any trial, so a head tremor can fire the first yes. The camera follows the head, not the eyes, so the first glance can miss. Fix: a practice round that coaches the position and tests one nod.

Other gaps in the interaction rules:

- R8 against R11: a late nod is silent for a product but spoken at checkout.
- R10 against R12: after a cancel, the code in view starts checkout again.
- R5 and R6: "in view" has no time threshold, so one dropped frame lets R6 arm the nod again.
- R3, R7, R11: a yes counts before the prompt ends, and R7 can swap the product under the nod. Fix: each intent carries the ID of the last finished prompt.
- No rule covers undo, help, camera restart, a spoken cart review, or a cart change during checkout.

What works:

- "Glance tells, nod buys": a blind shopper identifies a product at no cost.
- No countdown: silence never adds or pays.
- The voice never guesses a price or an allergen.
